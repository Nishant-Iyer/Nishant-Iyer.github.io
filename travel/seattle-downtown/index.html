Overview

Packed between Elliott Bay and the hilly neighborhoods to the east, Downtown Seattle unsurprisingly contains the city's bustling financial and retail district. This is also where many of Seattle's tourist attractions are, including the iconic Pike Place Market, the expansive Seattle Art Museum, the touristy waterfront, and some of the city's most stunning architecture, all within easy walking distance of each other.

    Understand

    After the Great Fire of 1889, Seattle's business district moved northward from Pioneer Square, just in time for the Klondike Gold Rush of 1897 to cement Seattle as the commercial center of the region. The geography of downtown transformed, as several of the hills around downtown were regraded, sluiced into Elliott Bay to create flat land for Seattle's explosive growth in the early 1900s. Most level ground in central Seattle today was once either a hillside or a tidal flat.

    The oldest buildings in downtown were built shortly after the Great Fire, but much of Seattle's skyline emerged since the 1960s, filling out the space between Smith Tower and the Space Needle at the district's boundaries.

    Get in

    By car

    Getting in by car is not recommended, due to the congestion and parking problems, but if you have to, these are the main routes to get in:

    • Interstate 5 (I-5) northbound: exit either onto James Street (Exit 164) or Seneca Street (Exit 165, on the left side of the road).
    • I-5 southbound: exit onto Stewart Street (Exit 166), Union Street (Exit 165B) or James Street (Exit 165A).
    • State Route 99 (SR-99) northbound: exit onto Alaskan Way S.
    • SR-99 southbound: exit onto Denny Way/Downtown.
    • Interstate 90 (I-90) from the Eastside: continue straight onto the 4th Avenue S exit, then turn left towards Downtown, or exit onto I-5 north and follow the I-5 northbound directions.
    • State Route 520 (SR-520) from the Eastside: exit onto I-5 south and follow the I-5 southbound directions.

    By ferry

    All downtown ferries and water taxis operate from Pier 52, the Colman Dock, or Pier 50 that adjoins it to the south.

    • Washington State Ferries (WSF) operates the Seattle / Bainbridge Island route and the Seattle / Bremerton route from Pier 52 (Colman Dock), for both drive on and walk on traffic. Both rides are fun and scenic.

    By public transit

    See Seattle#By bus for a comprehensive list of intercity bus service from outside the Seattle metropolitan area: the Olympic Peninsula, Spokane, Ellensburg, Wenatchee, Yakima and other parts of the state; Portland; and Vancouver, BC. Downtown is the hub of the region's public transit system. Almost every neighborhood in Seattle has a direct route to either downtown or the light rail network.

    Link light rail, operated by Sound Transit, runs under 3rd Ave with stops downtown at Westlake Station (5th & Pine, Westlake Mall), Symphony Station (3rd Ave & University St), and Pioneer Square Station (3rd & James St). Adult fares are a flat $3.00, regardless of distance; children 18 and under ride for free. A train runs every 8-15 minutes from 5:00 to midnight.

    • The 1 Line from Downtown Federal Way to the south via Seattle-Tacoma International Airport, Tukwila and South Seattle, and from Lynnwood City Center to the north via Mountlake Terrace, Shoreline, University of Washington and Capitol Hill.
    • The 2 Line from Downtown Redmond, Bellevue and Mercer Island to the east, and from Lynnwood with the same stops as the 1 Line.

    The Seattle Center Monorail is a functioning relic from the 1962 World's Fair that connects Westlake Center directly to the Seattle Center north of Downtown, home to the Space Needle. One-way tickets are $3.50 per adult, $1.75 children ages 6-18/seniors.

    King County Metro buses serve Seattle neighborhoods and the surrounding cities in the suburbs of King County. Buses board on 3rd Ave (both directions); 2nd Ave (SB); and 4th Ave (NB). They also board on some of the east-west streets. $2.75 at all times and on all routes. The senior (ages 65 or older) fare is $1.00. Youth (18 and under) ride free. Includes free transfers for a 2 hour period.

    The South Lake Union Streetcar connects Westlake Center to South Lake Union, just to the north. $2.25 per adult and $1 per senior, regardless of distance. Youth board for free. You must purchase a ticket or tap your ORCA card at one of the streetcar stops before boarding.

    The free Waterfront Shuttle connects Pioneer Square to the Space Needle mostly via Alaskan Way, the main boulevard along the waterfront. It runs during the summer (between Memorial Day in May and Labor Day in September), every 15 minutes from 10AM to 10PM.

    From outside the city

    • Sound Transit (ST) provides all-day express bus service from the outlying suburbs including Auburn (578), Bellevue (550), Issaquah (554), Redmond (545), Federal Way (577, 578), Dupont (592), Gig Harbor (595), Lakewood (592, 594) and Tacoma (590, 594, 595). Northbound ST buses board along 4th Ave between Jackson and Olive while southbound buses board along 2nd Ave from Stewart to Yesler.
    • The Sounder, a commuter rail service operated by Sound Transit, has lines running south to Lakewood via Tacoma (S-Line) and north to Everett (N-Line). The Sounder stops at King Street Station in the International District.
    • Community Transit (CT) Operates local and regional public transit bus routes in Snohomish County, except only local routes in Everett. Communities within the county served by this operator's routes include Arlington, Edmonds, Everett, Lynnwood, Marysville, Mill Creek, Monroe, Mountlake Terrace, Mukilteo, Snohomish, and Stanwood. From Downtown Seattle they operate express buses to Snohomish via Monroe & Woodinville (424). Other routes connect to the Link light rail 1 Line at Lynnwood, Shoreline North/185th Ave or Mountlake Terrace Stations. They operate the Sound Transit routes to Everett (510) and Lynnwood (515) during weekday afternoons and to Downtown Seattle during weekday mornings only.

    Get around

    Seattle's downtown is compact and dense. The street grid is skewed from the rest of the city, aligned with the waterfront instead of cardinal directions. Avenues (running NW to SE, parallel to the waterfront) are hill-free and can easily be walked. However, streets (running NE to SW) can be extremely steep. To help with the steep hills, many office towers have public escalators from one side of the block to the other.

    Buses run up and down the avenues nearly constantly, especially along 3rd Ave, which is closed to personal vehicles. Most routes are straight through downtown, so when your feet are tired, hop onto the next bus for a few blocks.

    A rule of thumb to remember the downtown street names, from Yesler Way to Westlake Park, is the mnemonic "Jesus Christ Made Seattle Under Protest", as the streets are named as six first-letter pairs of these words (Jefferson & James, Cherry & Columbia, Marion & Madison, Spring & Seneca, University & Union, Pike & Pine).

    See Sky View Observatory (Columbia Center)

    This observatory sits on the 73rd floor of the Columbia Center, one of the tallest buildings on the West Coast and the tallest public observation deck in the Pacific Northwest, sitting 932 feet (284.2 meters) above the ground and more than 330 feet taller than the Space Needle.     Waterfront

    The waterfront along downtown was once the hub of Seattle's maritime activity, and then was separated from the rest of downtown for decades by a concrete freeway. Today, this stretch of boardwalk between the Colman Ferry Dock and the Pier 66 cruise terminal is the Waterfront Park, a wide promenade lined with public art, souvenir shops, seafood vendors and views across the bustling Elliott Bay.

    Pike Place Market

    Pike Place Market is one of the oldest functioning public markets in the country. It consists of dozens of little shops tucked into a few square blocks downtown, across multiple haphazard levels. Even if you hate shopping you might still like this place, with its colorful atmosphere and quirky gimmicks, like the famous seafood stand where the staff toss fish from one end to the other. As the weather gets warmer, many artisans set up booths to sell photographs, glass, ceramics, and fresh flowers. Farmers come to sell their produce, and a vast amount of tiny hole-in-the-wall places offer all kinds of cuisine, from street food to high-end seafood to trendy bars. While it is one of Seattle's most touristy destinations, it stays true to its roots as a farmers market for local shoppers; its policies encourage farmers to regularly be present at their booths, craftspeople to make their own wares and businesses to be owner-operated.

    It's within walking distance of the waterfront, and crowds fill the market each time a cruise ship is parked in the harbor. On the brick-paved Pike Place that serves as a central plaza, look for big blond Johnny Hahn on his portable piano or one of the other regular street musicians. Head into the lower levels beneath the crowded main arcade to explore the cramped, dusty shops buried within the building. Beyond the main building, the market extends to a hodge-podge of small markets and gourmet restaurants along the narrow Post Alley, on the steep hill between Pike Place and 1st Ave.

    Westlake

    Seattle's conventional retail corridor stretches along Pike and Pine Streets from Pike Place to the Convention Center at 7th Ave, centered on Westlake Park and Westlake Station. This is where the city's department stores were once concentrated. The flagship Nordstrom department store dominates the high-end, supported by standalone stores for upscale-for-Seattle brands (more activewear than high fashion) like Arc'teryx, Uniqlo and Urban Outfitters.

    Stay safe

    Downtown Seattle has a sizable population of homeless people. While many beg for change and some seem unstable, only a few are actually dangerous. Be careful after dark in some areas around the downtown core, such as 3rd Ave between Pine St and Pike St, outside the King County Courthouse at 3rd Ave between James St and Jefferson St, and around Pioneer Square and the International District immediately south of Downtown.

    Go next

    • A short ride by rail or bus takes you to Pioneer Square and International District, the Georgetown neighborhood, and South Seattle.
    • A ride by the South Lake Union Streetcar or the monorail will take you to the districts of Queen Anne and South Lake Union
    • A short walk away to the other side of the Interstate 5 is the hip district of Capitol Hill.
