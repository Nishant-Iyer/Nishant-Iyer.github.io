Overview

Issaquah is a city of 40,000 people (2019) in King County in the Puget Sound region of Washington State. Its historic downtown is a popular base for hiking in the foothills of the Cascades.

By car

  • From Interstate 90 (I-90), take exit 15 (17th Ave NW) or exit 17 (Front Street).
  • From State Route 18, take Issaquah Hobart Road south of Tiger Mountain, which becomes Front St.
  • State Route 900 leaves Renton as Sunset Blvd NE, becomes Renton-Issaquah Road, then 17th Ave NW at the junction with I-5.
  • East Lake Sammamish Pkwy goes 12 mi along the east side of Lake Sammamish from Front St & I-90 () at Exit #17, to its intersection with SR-202 just east of downtown Redmond.
  • West Lake Sammamish Pkwy goes 10 mi along the west side of Lake Sammamish to Exit #13 of I-90 (Lakewood Blvd) just west of Issaquah.

By bicycle

Bike lanes run east-west along W Lake Sammamish Pkwy SE (north of I-90, preferred) and Newport Way NW (south of I-90).

The East Lake Sammamish Trail (along E Lake Sammamish Pkwy) is the best north-south way to downtown, as it bypasses one of Issaquah's busiest retail areas and the high-traffic interstate crossings.

Get around

The main east-west roads are Gilman Blvd and Newport Way. Front Street runs north-south through downtown.

Issaquah is best accessed by car, although bicycling is a viable option.

Downtown Issaquah is easy to explore on foot. Additionally, there a network of hiking and walking trails which extend through the Gilman Blvd shopping district to Lake Sammamish State Park. It is possible to walk from Issaquah to Redmond on this trail network.

By public transit

The transportation hub (bus station) in Issaquah is the Issaquah Transit Center on NW Maple St & 17th Ave NW. The Issaquah TC offers routes with service to Downtown Seattle (214, 554); UW in the U-District (271,556); Bellevue (214,271,556); Redmond (269,554); North Bend via Snoqualmie (208); and other surrounding cities (208,269,271,545,556).

By bicycle

Bike lanes run east-west on Gilman, though alternative routes (Newport Way, W. Lake Sammamish Pkwy -- see above) have less traffic. If you need to cross I-90, the best route is the pedestrian trail adjacent to 4th Ave NW. Front Street/Issaquah Pine Lake between Gilman and SE 56th street has extremely heavy vehicle traffic.

See

Olde Town, centered along Front Street, is historic downtown Issaquah. It is known for its mixture of art galleries, shops, unique restaurants and taverns, theaters, historical sites and museums, some dating back to the 1930s or earlier. It's where much of the festivities of Issaquah Salmon Days occur.

See Gilman Town Hall Museum

From the Gilman Town Hall Museum Website- "This exhibit tells the story of Issaquah's past through hundreds of photographs and artifacts and a variety of interactive elements. Come see one of Issaquah's original water pipes, a rare Native American fur trade knife, and graffiti hidden for 75 years inside the walls of the fish hatchery. Set off an imaginary charge with an authentic dynamite blaster, ring a logging camp bell or listen to the music of the Squak Valley Hot Shots."

Do Lake Sammamish State Park

Lake Sammamish State Park is a 512-acre day-use park with 6,858 feet of waterfront on Lake Sammamish. The area around the lake was an important culture zone for local Native American tribes for centuries. The park provides deciduous forest and wetland vegetation for the enjoyment of visitors. A salmon-bearing creek and a great-blue-heron rookery are additional features. The park includes diverse natural wetlands, a large great blue heron rookery and the salmon-bearing Issaquah Creek. The park has one of the largest freshwater beaches in the greater Seattle area.

Hiking

The Issaquah Alps consist of nearby Cougar, Squak and Tiger Mountains. Each mountain hosts a network of trails for all fitness levels. They are popular for having some of the closest hiking to Seattle, having the closest strenuous hikes to Seattle, and for staying below the snow level for most of the winter.

Squak Mountain is a state park, and Tiger Mountain is mostly run by WA Department of Natural Resources, both of which require a pass to access by vehicle. The Discover Pass is $10 a day, or $30 a year.

Do Cougar Mountain Regional Wildland Park

A 3000-acre park with 36 miles of hiking trails (12 miles suitable for equestrian use). It showcases a variety of natural features from forests and waterfalls to meadows and cliffs. There are four primary trailheads, each offering a different experience.

from I-90: take Exit 13 and drive south on Lakemont Blvd SE for 3.1 miles; look for the entrance to the Red Town Trailhead on the left side of the road💲 Free

Do Tiger Mountain State Forest

The easternmost of the Issaquah Alps. The NCRCA encompasses 4,430 acres that range in elevation from 470 feet above sea level at Tradition Plateau to 2,948 feet at the summit of West Tiger Peak 1, the highest of three peaks within the natural area. The City of Issaquah owns most of the Tradition Plateau that is adjacent to the NRCA. The city co-manages the land with the Natural Areas Program at the Washington State Department of Natural Resources (DNR).

drive east on I-90 past Bellevue; just past Issaquah, take exit 20; turn right on 270th Ave SE; turn right on SE 79th Street; drive through the gate, onto the gravel road and into the parking lot💲 Discover Pass required for vehicles

Shop Gilman Village

An Issaquah landmark for almost half a century, Gilman Village is a mixed use center featuring shops, restaurants, personal services, even a preschool, all residing in houses, buildings and other structures preserved from Issaquah's historic past. Good for a casual walk with a variety of unique stores and restaurants to visit, from Greek, Mexican, Indian, and American food, The Black Duck cask and bottle house, plus craft art galleries, antique stores, home decor, clothing, home goods, all independently owned and locally charming.

Drink Formula Brewing

Local microbrewery with pizza kitchen and large outdoor space. The roomy, modern atmosphere of this Issaquah brewery combines artisan pizza varieties with equally high quality beers for diverse palates, from citrus-forward IPAs, to lagers from Latin to German styles, to write-home-about stouts, sours, and fruit beers.
