Overview

Lolo is a community of about 4,400 people in northwestern Montana, about 10 mi from Missoula. It is at the eastern end of the Lolo Trail, a route through a challenging section of the Rockies used by the Nez Perce people and by Lewis and Clark, who camped here in 1805 and again in 1806. Today this route leads travellers to hot springs and untamed wilderness.

    Understand

    The area around the major city (for Montana) of Missoula is growing fast, and Lolo bears the marks of more people added quickly. It's laid back but can feel crowded in the busy summer months. The Missoula area is more liberal than the rest of the state.

    The town is at the eastern end of the Lolo Trail. This historic trail, connected the Bitterroot Valley in Montana and the Weippe Prairie in Idaho. Known as naptnišaqs, or "Nez Perce Trail" in Salish, was used by Nez Perce people in the 18th century, and by the Lewis and Clark Expedition, guided by Old Toby of the Shoshone, on their westward snowbound journey in September 1805. The Lolo Trail is a National Historic Landmark, designated for its importance to the Lewis and Clark Expedition, and its role in the 1877 Nez Perce War.

    Get in

    By plane

    Missoula Montana Airport (MSO) in Missoula receives flights from Denver, Colorado; Salt Lake City, Utah; Seattle/Tacoma, Washington; Minneapolis/St Paul, Minnesota; Dallas/Fort Worth, Texas; Phoenix/Mesa, Arizona; and other cities.

    By car

    U.S. Route 93 (US-93) travels through the town, north from Hamilton and south from Missoula.

    U.S. Route 12 (US-12) intersects the town on the western side, over Lolo Pass from North Central Idaho.

    Get around

    It is highly recommended to have a car, though you could walk or bike through town.

    See and do

    Get outside and enjoy hiking, rafting, "tubing", rock climbing, skiing, fishing, or golfing. This area is known for its active lifestyle.

    Do Traveler's Rest State Park

    A 51-acre (21-ha) park. Significant archeological findings made in 2002, including latrine sites with traces of mercury and fire hearths, make this the only site on the Lewis and Clark National Historic Trail that has yielded physical proof of the explorers' presence. This spot is largely unchanged from the days of Lewis and Clark. From this location, Lewis and Clark split up to explore Montana during their return trip, not reuniting until they reached Sanish, North Dakota.

    More adventurous soakers may prefer to hike out to some natural hot springs just across the Idaho border (about 50 minutes from Lolo).

    Do Jerry Johnson Hot Springs

    The springs themselves are less than a mile hike in, and are large enough to accommodate a couple of dozen people. These springs are also clothing optional. If the springs are packed, as they often are, try hiking a quarter-mile further down the trail to two smaller hot springs.

    Do Weir Hot Spring

    A smaller hot spring about a mile in on a narrow and hilly trail, which can be treacherously icy in the winter and spring. Although the drive is longer and the hike harder, visitors to Weir are compensated by smaller crowds than at Jerry Johnson. In addition, the Forest Service allows camping at Weir, unlike at Jerry Johnson.
