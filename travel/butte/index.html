Overview

Butte in southwest Montana, "The Richest Hill on Earth" from around 1880 to 1920, was the largest and most notorious copper boom town in the American West, home to hundreds of saloons and a famous red-light district. The city still flaunts its legacy of industry, wealth and squalor. As the sign along I-15 states: "Butte has a most cosmopolitan population derived from the four corners of the world. She was a bold, unashamed, rootin', tootin' hell-roarin' camp in days gone by and still drinks her liquor straight."

Food for you

Butte is known for the food of its Irish-influenced mining heritage. Pasties (PAS-tees) are a semicircle of pastry dough, stuffed with a mix of meat, potato and onion, baked and served smothered with brown gravy. Miners took these hand pies down into the shafts in the morning, and used their helmet candles to heat the self-contained lunch. The distinctive crimped edge was used to handle the pastry with dirty hands, then discarded at the end of the meal. (Go ahead and eat it if you haven't been working in the mine.) Pasties are served locally at restaurants.

    Understand

    The rock underneath and surrounding Butte is honeycombed with drifts, winzes and stopes that extend beneath the city. There are over 3000 miles of shafts that reach a depth of over a mile. These mines have produced billions of dollars worth of gold, silver, zinc, and above all else, copper. For decades, about one-quarter of global copper supply was extracted from here.

    It used to be Montana's largest city, but has dwindled in size and importance along with the state's mining industry. As of 2020, it has a population of under 35,000. The city is still trying to reinvent itself without intensive mining as its focal point, but it has a century of well-preserved heritage to build upon. As cities like Missoula and Bozeman become popular with out-of-staters and expensive for long-time residents, Butte is still a city for Montanans.

    History

    Butte started as a mining camp during a 1864 Montana gold rush. There was enough gold and silver here to keep the town humming along until the electrification of the U.S. in the 1880s created a huge demand for copper, making Butte the world's largest copper boom town. Employment opportunities in the mines attracted surges of European and Asian immigrants, who helped to make Butte the largest city between Seattle and Minneapolis. Over the next hundred years, Butte's mining and smelting operations generated more than $48 billion worth of ore.

    Industrial magnates fought for control of Butte's mining wealth. These "Copper Kings" were William A. Clark, Marcus Daly, James Andrew Murray and F. Augustus Heinze. Daly's Anaconda Copper Mining Company quickly came to dominate the local industry, eventually owning all the mines on Butte Hill. Clark used his wealth to pivot into railroads and politics, establishing Las Vegas in the eponymous Clark County. As mining consolidated and industrialized, the city became a center for labor unions and socialist politics, famously leading to the labor riot of 1914.

    By the 1950s, labor costs and international competition lead to a transition from underground to open-pit mining. Thousands of homes were destroyed to excavate the Berkeley Pit, that large "lake" in the northeast corner of town you see on modern maps. Anaconda Copper opened it in 1954 as the largest truck-operated open pit copper mine in the nation. By the 1970s, copper mining had hit a prolonged bust that shuttered most of the mines, ending Butte's heyday.

    See Our Lady of the Rockies

    Our Lady of the Rockies is a 90-foot (27 m) statue, dedicated to the Blessed Virgin Mary, that sits atop the Continental Divide overlooking Butte, Montana. It is the second tallest statue in the United States after The Statue of Liberty. The statue was built by volunteers using donated materials to honor women everywhere, especially mothers. The base is 8,510 feet above sea level and 3,500 feet above the town. The statue is lit and visible at night.

    Uptown Historic District

    Uptown, named for its steep streets, is on a hillside on the northwestern edge of the town. It is one of the largest National Historic Landmark Districts in the U.S., containing nearly 6,000 contributing properties. The thousands of historic commercial and residential buildings from the boom times give it an old-fashioned appearance, with an abundance of lavish Victorian homes and Queen Anne style cottages built in the late 19th century.

    See Copper King Mansion

    34-room Victorian mansion built as the Butte residence of William Andrews Clark, one of Montana's three famous "Copper Kings". The home features fresco painted ceilings, elegant parquets of rare imported wood, gas and electric chandeliers, ornate hand-carved fireplaces and stairways and stained-glass windows. They offer 90 minute tours during the day, as well as overnight stays as a bed and breakfast.

    Fourteen headframes (sometimes called gallows frames or gallus frames) still stand over mine shafts in Butte, from the hundreds that once peppered the horizon. These timber and steel structures once hoisted miners and equipment into the thousand-feet-deep mines, from the late 1800s into the mid-1900s. They have become a proud symbol of the city. Several headframes are illuminated with LED lights at night. They're all fenced off, but you can get close to some of them.

    Events

    Do Saint Patrick's Day

    The Saint Patrick's Day celebration is a regional legend. It is the kind of party that parents warn their kids about. Butte is one of those rare places that does not have an open container law. Butte is heavily Irish to this day, and the local heritage extends into the enthusiasm for this holiday. Beer is colored green, and many people flock to the town for drinking and partying.
