Overview

Big Sur contains vast wildernesses and breathtaking views as it stretches 72 miles along the rugged Central Coast of California. The area is great for outdoor recreation and contains several state parks, two national wilderness areas, and is part of the Los Padres National Forest, all of which include hiking trails. It is the most remote and scenic stretch of the Pacific Coast Highway, though the twisty and exposed road is not an easy drive. The name during the Spanish colonial period was "el país grande del sur", meaning "the big country of the south", referring to the vast relatively unexplored region south of the historic capital of Monterey. It was anglicized as Big Sur by European settlers in 1915.

While the region has no specific borders, most residents consider Big Sur to be bounded on the north by Malpaso Creek, approximately 129 miles south of San Francisco, and on the south by San Carpóforo Canyon, 244 miles north of Los Angeles.

    Understand

    Big Sur begins a few miles south of Carmel and includes the small settlements of Palo Colorado Canyon, Big Sur Village (between Andrew Molera State Park and Pfeiffer Big Sur State Park), Posts, Lucia, and Gorda. It ends near San Simeon (where the Hearst Castle is located).

    The Santa Lucia Mountains dominate the landscape, characterized by extremely steep slopes emerging right from the shoreline, dissected by deep stream canyons. The region was hard to access over land, and even harder to access by sea, so few people ever came here until the effort to build the highway. As it was left largely untouched for so long, it retained its uncommon beauty, and is now among the most popular destinations in California, rivaling the national parks in numbers of visitors.

    History

    Three Native American tribes - the Ohlone, Esselen, and Salinan - are believed to have lived in Big Sur as hunter-gatherers prior to the arrival of Europeans. The Spanish explorer Juan Cabrillo was the first European to view the area when he sailed along the coast in 1542. Spaniards arrived on foot in 1769, but the high cliffs and rough terrain forced them inland. Monterey was settled in 1770, during which the Governor made only two lands grants in the area, Rancho El Sur and Rancho San Jose y Sur Chiquito. The ranchos were maintained by caretakers.

    Mexico took control of the area when it gained independence from Spain in 1821, and in 1848 Mexico ceded California to the United States following the Mexican-American War. By 1862, several pioneers had moved into the area, although the inaccessibility of the area prevented any significant development. Many places are named after these early settlers, most prominently after members of the Pfeiffer family, who donated parcels to the state at various times.

    The construction of the present-day Highway 1 began in 1919 using convict labor. Construction stopped in 1924 when funds were exhausted and resumed in 1928. Construction required 33 bridges to be built and thousands of tons of dynamite to blast the road through the coastal cliffs. The $10 million, unpaved Carmel-San Simeon Highway was finally opened in 1937. Over the years, Monterey County lawmakers and local residents have established extremely tough development restrictions, making it illegal to build almost anything within view of the highway and other public points. Fewer than 2,000 residents live on the coast year-round.

    Flora and fauna

    Much of the landscape consists of open, chaparral-covered hills. Surface water is scarce, so when you approach one of the many narrow stream-laden valleys emptying into the coast, you will suddenly find yourself in a shady pocket of lush woodlands. Big Sur is the southernmost range of the world's tallest tree, the coast redwood (Sequoia sempervirens), taking full advantage of the frequent fog that softens the dry conditions. Thanks to the remoteness of the region, many of the existing groves have never been logged.

    The critically-endangered California condor was reintroduced to the region in 1997, and as of 2014 nearly three dozen condors make the area their home. Mountain lions, though rarely seen by human eyes, roam throughout the Big Sur region. Raptors in the area include bald eagles and peregrine falcons.

    Once thought to be extinct in California, a colony of 60 sea otters was discovered near Bixby Bridge in 1938. Today sea otters are frequently seen in the area, as well as sea lions, elephant seals and harbor seals. Orcas patrol the coast year-round, while other whale species are seasonal: humpback whales can be seen from April through December, blue whales from June through October, and gray whales from December through May. The abundance of seals does not go unnoticed by sharks, including the great white shark, which patrol the waters but will only be seen by very lucky visitors.

    Climate

    Along the coast the climate is moderate year-round, although further inland temperatures are significantly warmer in summer and cooler in winter. Thick, dense fog blankets the coast during the summer, and while it usually burns off during the day, it can occasionally linger and make travel along Highway 1 treacherous.

    Get in

    The easiest and most common way to get to Big Sur is by car. There are only two roads in:

    • California State Highway 1 (also known as the Pacific Coast Highway) winds through Big Sur flanked by the steep Santa Lucia Mountains to the east and the rocky Pacific Coast to the west. The highway is prone to rock and mud slides which have been so severe in the past that the highway has sometimes closed for over a year. Construction can also cause delays or closures, so check California highway road conditions prior to setting out.
    • The winding, narrow 25-mile-long Nacimiento-Fergusson Road from Highway 101 and Jolon to Mill Creek is the only alternative route.

    There are only four gas stations along the route, and fuel is expensive, so fill your tank before entering the Big Sur region. Gas along the highway can be several dollars per gallon more expensive than in less remote parts of the state.

    From San Francisco, take US-101 south to CA-156 west, which merges with Highway 1 at Castroville, 20 miles from Malpaso Canyon. Approximately 129 miles and a 2- to 3-hour drive.

    From Los Angeles, take US-101 north to San Luis Obispo and exit onto Highway 1 toward Morro Bay/Hearst Castle, about 45 miles south of San Carpóforo Canyon. Approximately 244 miles and a 4- to 5-hour drive.

    Get around

    Highway 1 through Big Sur is a long, narrow, winding road, so those prone to car sickness may want to take medication prior to setting out. There is only one lane in each direction, with no passing lanes. Most of the road is very limited in width. Paved shoulders are narrow and often disappear in steep areas. Driving in the dark, in fog, or in rainy conditions can be quite dangerous due to the risk of a collision or of straying off the highway.

    While the speed limit is 55 mph (90 km/h) unless otherwise posted, expect speeds of 35-45 mph for most of the route, and as slow as 15-20 mph on some of the sharpest curves, or due to congestion. Given the many sharp turns, passing can be extremely dangerous. If there are five or more cars following you, courtesy and California law require you to pull over at a safe point and allow them to pass. Use one of the many unpaved, wide pull outs along the route. This allows you and others to enjoy a safe drive.

    Big Sur Village is a mile-long portion of the highway containing gas stations, roadside markets, lodges and restaurants, but services elsewhere in the region are very limited. The small communities of Posts, Lucia, Plaskett, and Gorda are south of Big Sur Village, but these settlements generally have little more than a single inn or restaurant available for travelers.

    By bicycle

    Bicycling along Highway 1 is popular, though extremely challenging given the narrow or non-existent shoulders, constant elevation changes, and precipitous cliffs. Cyclists will also be dealing with motorists who are likely distracted by the scenery, dealing with fog, or unaccustomed to sharing a narrow highway.

    See

    You can stop to admire the scenery at one of the many dirt turnouts and vista points along the route. When stopping at a viewpoint be sure that your vehicle is entirely off the roadway.

    The turnout viewpoints are free, but going deeper into most parks requires a fee. A day pass for California State Parks costs $10 per vehicle, and covers all state parks visited that day. Sites run by the U.S. Forest Service charge a separate fee, and don't take the America the Beautiful (Interagency) Passes that most other federal sites accept.

    See Bixby Bridge

    Iconic bridge built in 1933. One of the most photographed in the world because of its location within such beautiful scenery. The concrete arch spans 320 ft, and its 280 ft height makes it one of the tallest single-span concrete bridges in the world. You cannot stop at or anywhere near Bixby Bridge to take pictures. Parking on the bridge itself has always been illegal; effective August 5, 2026, Monterey County (with the state government's consent) has imposed a one-year moratorium on parking anywhere within 900 feet north of the bridge or 700 feet south of the bridge. "No Parking" signs have been posted. This was because of extreme overtourism, as many tourists were not allowing a critical shortage of parking spaces stop them from getting their desired photographs. They were increasingly parking and leaving their vehicles right in the middle of Highway 1, which was illegal, dangerous, and resulted in heavy traffic congestion. The one-year parking moratorium is intended to alleviate that issue while the county government studies long-term solutions.

    Hiking and backpacking

    There are many possible day hikes varying in length and difficulty. You can hike to beaches and vistas along the coast, along rivers and through canyons, and through redwood forests in the Santa Lucia Mountains. For longer and more remote adventures, backpacking is an option. There are hundreds of miles of trails through the region, particularly the Ventana Wilderness.

    Always check conditions before hiking or backpacking.

    Do Ventana Wilderness

    The wilderness covers 240,026 acres. Within the wilderness area alone there are 237 miles of trails and 55 designated camping areas. As of Oct 2026, the Pine Ridge trail which begins in Big Sur is closed due to the Timber and Plaskett Fires. As an alternative, consider the Mt. Manual Trail into the Little Sur River watershed. It is accessible from within Pfeiffer Big Sur State Park. A second alternative is the North Coast Ridge Road trail which is accessible from behind the Ventana Inn. Both are strenuous uphill climbs, gaining 3,000 feet in elevation in less than four miles.

    Beaches

    Big Sur is lined with remote and pristine beaches. The highway is typically a few hundred feet up the steep coastline, which means most beaches require a bit of a hike to get to. Andrew Molera State Park, Pfeiffer Beach, and Sand Dollar Beach are the most commonly visited, as they are easier to access.

    Do Andrew Molera State Park

    Miles of trails, beaches, and meadows. The 24 primitive camp sites were closed "indefinitely" in 2021.

    Do Pfeiffer Beach

    Pfeiffer Beach is known for the huge rock towers that emerge from the water, one of which has a natural keyhole through which the sunset can be viewed at the right time of year. The sand can have a purple hue due to manganese garnet (dig down a bit to find it). Swimming is possible although there is a strong undertow. Sycamore Canyon Rd is unmarked and easy to miss from Highway 1 (it will be the only paved and non-gated road to the west in the area). The winding road is one-lane and shared with local residents, and the parking area only accommodates sixty vehicles and is often full. You can park at the Pine Ridge Trailhead on Highway 1 and walk.

    Camping

    Camping is popular in Big Sur and there are many small campgrounds along Highway 1 in addition to those listed below.

    Monterey County, which includes nearly all of the Big Sur route, prohibits roadside camping and overnight parking along any paved or dirt road, although this restriction does not apply to tired drivers needing a brief rest. It is illegal to camp anyplace other than designated campgrounds. Fires are only permitted in established campgrounds. Illegal campfires can result in arrest and a stiff fine.

    Stay Big Sur Campground and Cabins

    Accommodations include campsites, tent-cabins and rustic cabins, all beneath giant redwoods and beside the Big Sur river. There is great swimming in the river and hiking is close by. Kids can ride inner-tubes or rubber boats down the river and a 3-mile drive either north or south will get you to beautiful beaches (Pfieffer to the south and Molera to the north.)

    Stay safe

    Highway 1 dangers

    Highway 1 through Big Sur is a narrow, winding two-lane road which in many places was carved out of steep cliff faces. Driving Highway 1 can be a delightful challenge. However, it requires your complete attention at all times. Getting distracted for just a second can be fatal.

    Caltrans provides guardrails only for the most dangerous sheer cliffs, where any deviation from the roadway means certain death. Instead, many dropoffs are separated only by small earthen berms or ridges. Berms enable visitors to enjoy less obstructed views of Big Sur's gorgeous scenery. But if you allow your vehicle's tires to drift off the highway, the adjacent berm is unlikely to stop you from flying over the top and off the cliff on the other side. Numerous drivers have lost their lives that way. If all of this makes you nervous, consider driving the route northbound to stay on the less exposed side.

    Always check traffic conditions before you go. Try to avoid driving long distances after dark as fog and the many sharp turns can severely restrict your visibility. In the winter, sudden landslides can block the road entirely or cause major backups. For example, in May 2017, a major landslide south of Gorda closed the road to through traffic for many months. Construction can reduce the roadway to a single lane, controlled by traffic lights.

    Go next

    • Carmel - The northern boundary of Big Sur is the town of Carmel, located 20 miles from Big Sur Village. Carmel is a beautiful oceanside town that is home to the historic Mission San Carlos Borromeo de Carmelo, several large beaches, excellent restaurants and an abundance of upscale lodging. It borders the iconic Pebble Beach golf course and is the entry point to the 17-mile drive and its dramatic ocean views.
    • San Simeon - Marking the southern end of the Big Sur region and lying 80 miles south of Big Sur Village, San Simeon is home to Hearst Castle, the impressive residence of the former media tycoon William Randolph Hearst, now managed as a state park with tours available daily. A massive colony of more than 20,000 elephant seals can be viewed onshore just north of town every winter.
    • Make a loop with U.S. Highway 101. It's less scenic but much faster if you need to return to your starting point.
