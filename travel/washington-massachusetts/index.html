Overview

Washington is small rural town in the east central Berkshire Hills of Massachusetts. Washington mostly offers outdoor wilderness activities, including at October Mountain State Forest. It also has several historic buildings.

Understand

The area that is now Washington is historically Mohican land, near the Pocumtuc. Europeans colonized the area in 1760 and the town was incorporated in 1777. The town went by various names, including Greenoch, Watsontown and Hartville, before being renamed for George Washington in 1784. The town has always been very rural and very small in population. Most of the town area is forest wilderness. Washington is bordered by Dalton and Hinsdale to the north, Peru to the northeast, Middlefield to the east, Becket to the south, Lee to the southwest, Lenox to the west, and Pittsfield to the northwest. The majority of the 16,500 acres of October Mountain State Forest is in Washington. The Appalachian Trail also passes through the town.

Get in

By car

There are two main routes into Washington, both of which run north-south: MA Rt 8, from Hindsale to Becket, and Washington Mt Rd, from Dalton/Pittsfield to Becket. Washington Mt Rd begins on the Dalton/Pittsfield end at the corner junction of Williams St, and Dalton Division Rd, runs diagonally along the border of October Mountain State Forest, and turns into McNerny Rd in Becket. Washington Mt Rd can be difficult, even impassable, in bad winter conditions.

On foot

Washington lies on the Appalachian Trail and can be entered by it via the trailheads at street intersections. Route 8 and Washington Mt Rd can be traversed by foot, but this is long and difficult.

Get around

Car is the feasible option to get around Washington. If you are a good hiker, you can traverse the town on foot. MA Rt 8 is fairly passable on bicycle.

See Lower Historic District

A National Register of Historic Places property. Consists of four homesteads dating from 1765 to 1803, built along a stretch of Washington Mountain Road near the border with Becket. It includes the town's oldest house, that of William Milliken. The specific houses and address are as follows: Richmond - Foster - Corey House, 1570 Washington Mt Rd; Clark - Strong - Abbott - Pomeroy Farm, 1648 Washington Mt Rd; Hiram Savery Farm, 1651 Washington Mt Rd; and William Millekean Farm, 1796 Washington Mt Rd.

See Philip Eames House

An 1843 house built out of irregularly cut granite. It was built by Philip Eames, who owned several sawmills in the area. The National Register of Historic Places listing calls it "most imposing 19th century residence" of the town. The building has Federal and Greek Revivalist stylings.

See Saint Andrew's Chapel

Designed by Pittsfield architect George C. Harding and completed in 1899. Built for George Crane's adjacent summer estate. It only held services when Cranes were staying at the estate. Both the chapel and the Crane estate were eventually transferred to the Episcopal diocese. The chapel is in Late Gothic Revivalist style and is on the National Register of Historic Places.

See Upper Valley Historic District

A cluster of historic buildings in the area of the old town center, listed on the National Register of Historic Places. Good examples of Federal and Greek Revival architecture. The 1783 animal pound, a rectangular stone building, is the oldest extant structure. The town's current cemetery on the east side of Washington Mountain Road dates from 1805 and contains a few burials moved from the town's nearby first burying ground. The town hall, next to the cemetery, is from 1848/49 and sees occasional use for civic events. The two oldest houses in this district are the Elijah Crane House, from c. 1785–89, and the Jasper Morgan Tavern, from c. 1810–11.
