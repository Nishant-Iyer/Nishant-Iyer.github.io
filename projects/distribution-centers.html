Global distribution center placement

Where should a company put its warehouses to stay close to the most customers? I compared three ways of answering that across the world's 1,000 largest cities, and found that the "exact" solver was answering a smaller question than it seemed.

The problem

Pick k cities to host distribution centers so that customers, weighted by how much they buy, are as close as possible to their nearest center. Operations research calls this the p-median problem. It's easy to state and hard to solve exactly: choosing 5 sites from 1,000 cities has more than 8 trillion combinations.

The tool also lets a planner weight demand by population or by purchasing power, penalize sites in countries with weak logistics (the World Bank's Logistics Performance Index), and force specific cities to be included.

Three approaches

  1. K-Means: cluster cities in 3D space, then place each center at the city nearest its cluster's middle. Very fast, but it optimizes squared straight-line distance rather than the travel distance we care about.
  2. K-Medoids: work directly with distances along the Earth's surface. Start with a reasonable set of centers, then keep moving any center to any other city if that lowers total distance, until no move helps.
  3. Mixed-integer programming (MILP): write the problem as equations and hand it to a solver that proves its answer is optimal, but only over a list of candidate sites.

The "exact" solver never won. To keep it fast, it only allowed the 50 most populous cities as sites, and the best sites are often smaller, well-placed cities. Letting it consider more candidates closed the gap (1,609 km with 50, 1,569 with 100, 1,565 with 400) but took 74 seconds at 400. K-Medoids considers all 1,000 cities and reached 1,560 km in under a second. The lesson: an optimality guarantee only covers the problem you actually gave the solver.

Two bugs I fixed

  • Centers drifting toward the equator. K-Means averages points in 3D, and the average of points on a sphere sits inside it. The code turned that inner point straight into latitude without pushing it back to the surface, which pulled every center toward the equator. Fixing it cut the 5-center distance from 1,634 to 1,581 km.
  • A search that stopped early and changed every run. K-Medoids quit after 50 swaps instead of when no swap helped, and started from an unseeded random choice, so two runs could give different answers. It now applies the best swap each round until none helps, from a fixed seed.

Known limitation. GDP per person and logistics scores are filled in for 36 countries; the rest use placeholder values. That matters most for the purchasing-power and logistics options, and filling in full World Bank data is the obvious next step.

What I'd do next

  • Seed the MILP from K-Medoids. Use the fast answer to pick a smart candidate list, then let the exact solver confirm or improve it.
  • Use road and shipping distances instead of straight lines over the globe, at least within continents.
  • Add capacity and cost. Real centers have limits and different running costs; the MILP already supports capacities, and that's where it would earn its keep.
